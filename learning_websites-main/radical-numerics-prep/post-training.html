Part 1

Post-training, from basics to the interview

A companion to the main brief, focused on the one thing this role is actually about. It builds the whole post-training toolbox from first principles — SFT, reward models, RLHF, DPO and its cousins, rejection sampling, RLAIF, verifiable-reward RL, distillation — then lays out the trade-offs and the "when would you reach for this" decisions, and finally translates all of it into how Radical Numerics post-trains a biological model and how to answer the questions they will ask.

The one-sentence definition to lead with

Pretraining teaches a model what is likely (next-token prediction over a huge corpus); post-training teaches it what is wanted — turning a raw predictor into a system that is useful, steerable, honest about uncertainty, and good at the specific thing you ship. The posting's own words: the work "that turns a powerful base model into a system that is useful, steerable, robust, and scientifically productive."

1.1 Why a separate phase exists at all

A base model trained only to predict the next token is a distribution-matcher. Ask it a question and it will often continue with more questions, because that is a plausible continuation of the text. It has enormous latent capability but no preference over how to use it. Three gaps motivate every post-training method below:

  • Format / behavior gap. The model can do the task but doesn't know it is supposed to. Fixed by showing it examples of the desired behavior — supervised finetuning.
  • Preference / quality gap. Among many valid outputs, some are better, and "better" is easier to judge than to write down. Fixed by learning from comparisons — reward models, RLHF, DPO.
  • Correctness gap. For tasks with a checkable answer, you don't need human taste at all — you need the model to find the answer that passes the check. Fixed by RL against a verifier — RLVR/GRPO.

Almost every technique is a different answer to "where does the training signal come from, and how expensive is it?" Keep that question in your head; it is the axis the whole field is organized on, and it is exactly the axis that changes when your annotator is a wet lab instead of a human.

Part 2

The pipeline and where the stages sit

Modern model development is three regimes, not two. The middle one — mid-training (also called continued pretraining or annealing) — matters a lot here because Radical Numerics uses it heavily (context extension to 1M bp, adding whole new modalities).

Vocabulary you should use precisely

Alignment is the goal (making the model do what we want); post-training is the phase; SFT / RLHF / DPO / RLVR are methods within it. "Fine-tuning" is an overloaded word — it can mean full-parameter updates, SFT specifically, or a PEFT method like LoRA. Say which you mean.

Part 3

SFT — supervised fine-tuning, the foundation

The simplest and most important post-training method. Collect examples of the input paired with the output you want, and train with the ordinary cross-entropy loss — but only on the response tokens. It is behavior cloning: imitate the demonstrations.

# SFT loss — maximize likelihood of the target response y given prompt x L_SFT(θ) = − Σt log πθ( yt | x, y<t ) # loss masked to response tokens only

What it is good at

  • Teaching format and behavior. Instruction following, a response style, a tool-call schema, a new output head. Fast, stable, cheap to debug.
  • Teaching a new domain or modality when you have demonstrations. This is the bulk of what "adapting Omnii to antibodies" looks like before any preference step.
  • Being the required first step. Preference methods assume the model already produces reasonable candidates; SFT is what gets it there.

Where it falls down — and why the other methods exist

  • It can only imitate, never exceed, the demonstrations. The ceiling is your data. If no human (or no cheap process) can produce the ideal output, SFT cannot learn it.
  • It has no notion of "better." Every demonstration is treated as equally correct; it can't learn from a good-vs-bad comparison, and it never sees what a bad answer looks like.
  • Exposure bias / distribution shift. Trained on ground-truth prefixes, it must run on its own (imperfect) prefixes at inference — small errors compound.
  • Over-fitting to surface form and catastrophic forgetting of pretrained ability if you push too hard (see Part 12).
PEFT in one breath (likely a follow-up)

Full fine-tuning updates all weights. LoRA / QLoRA freeze the base and train small low-rank adapter matrices — ~0.1–1% of params — which is cheaper, resists forgetting, and is swappable per task. The trade-off: slightly lower ceiling than full FT on hard adaptations. For adding a modality to a large genomic model, a common pattern is new embeddings/heads trained fully, trunk at a low LR or via adapters — which is essentially what the 5hmC diagnostics result describes.

Part 4

Reward modeling — turning judgment into a number

When "good" is easier to recognize than to demonstrate, collect comparisons: show a labeler two responses to the same prompt and ask which is better. A reward model (RM) is trained to assign a scalar score that agrees with those preferences, using the Bradley–Terry model of pairwise choice.

# Bradley–Terry: probability the "chosen" response beats the "rejected" one L_RM(φ) = − log σ( rφ(x, ychosen) − rφ(x, yrejected) ) # σ = logistic

The RM is usually the base model with the output layer replaced by a single scalar head. Once trained it is a cheap, queryable proxy for the expensive labeler — which is exactly what you need to drive RL or to rank a batch of candidates.

The failure mode to name: reward hacking

An RM is a learned, imperfect stand-in for what you actually care about. Optimize against it hard enough and the policy finds inputs where the RM is wrong but confident — longer answers, sycophancy, formatting tricks. This is Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure. Mitigations: a KL penalty to the reference model, ensembles of RMs, periodic re-collection of fresh comparisons, and keeping a held-out judge. In biology the analogue is a surrogate fitness model the generator learns to fool — same disease, different clothes.

Part 5

RLHF / PPO — optimizing a policy against a reward

The classic three-step recipe (InstructGPT): (1) SFT, (2) train a reward model from comparisons, (3) use RL — usually PPO — to update the policy to maximize reward, with a KL penalty keeping it close to the SFT reference so it doesn't drift into reward-hacked nonsense.

# RLHF objective: maximize reward, stay near the reference policy maxθ Ex, y~πθ [ rφ(x, y) ] − β · KL( πθ(y|x) || πref(y|x) )

When RLHF/PPO is the right tool

  • The quality you want genuinely exceeds what demonstrations capture, and you can afford the complexity.
  • You want on-policy improvement — the model learning from its own samples, scored as they come.
  • You have (or can build) a reliable reward model, and a KL leash you trust.

Its operational pain — four models, instability, cost — is exactly what motivated DPO, the next section.

Part 6

DPO and the preference-optimization family

6.1 DPO itself

Direct Preference Optimization is the key insight that there is a closed-form relationship between the optimal RLHF policy and the reward. That lets you skip the explicit reward model and the RL loop entirely, and train directly on preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss. The policy is its own implicit reward model.

# DPO: raise the margin between chosen and rejected, measured against a frozen reference L_DPO = − log σ( β log [πθ(yw|x) / πref(yw|x)] − β log [πθ(yl|x) / πref(yl|x)] ) # y_w = preferred (win), y_l = rejected (lose)

Read it plainly: push up the model's (length-normalized, reference-relative) likelihood of the preferred response and push down the rejected one, with β controlling how far you're allowed to move from the reference. Two models in memory (policy + frozen reference), no sampling loop, stable, trains like supervised learning.

DPO advantages
  • Dramatically simpler and more stable than PPO — no RM, no critic, no on-policy rollouts.
  • Cheaper: two models, standard supervised tooling.
  • Strong results for preference tuning; the default first reach for most teams now.
DPO limitations
  • Off-policy by default — it learns from a fixed pair set, not from its own current samples, so it can't explore. Iterative/online DPO (regenerate pairs from the latest policy) narrows the gap to RLHF.
  • Sensitive to pair quality and to how far apart the pair is; noisy or near-tied pairs hurt.
  • Can reduce the likelihood of both responses (it only cares about the margin) and can overfit; variants below address this.

6.2 The variants, and what each one fixes

MethodWhat it changesReach for it when
DPOBaseline direct preference loss vs a frozen reference. You have clean preference pairs and want simple, stable tuning.
IPOAdds a regularizer that stops DPO over-fitting when preferences are near-deterministic (margins blowing up). Pairs are nearly always one-sided, or you see the model collapsing.
KTOLearns from unpaired binary good/bad labels (Kahneman–Tversky utility), no matched pairs required. You can label a single sample "worked / didn't" but can't easily form pairs — common with assay hits.
ORPOFolds preference into the SFT loss (an odds-ratio penalty), so one stage, no separate reference model. You want to combine demonstration + preference signal and minimize stages.
SimPOReference-free, length-normalized reward; drops the frozen reference entirely. Memory is tight or length bias is a problem.
cDPO / rDPORobustified DPO that models label noise explicitly. You know a fraction of your preference labels are wrong (assay noise).
Interview-ready framing

Don't recite the zoo. Say: "DPO is my default for preference tuning because it removes the reward model and the RL instability; I go to KTO when I have binary outcomes rather than pairs, IPO when preferences are near-deterministic and DPO over-fits, and I keep iterative/online variants in mind when off-policy staleness starts to bite." That shows you organize methods by the problem they solve, not by their acronyms.

Part 7

Rejection sampling / Best-of-N — the quiet workhorse

Generate many candidates, score them with a reward model or verifier, and keep the best. That is Best-of-N at inference time; feed those best samples back as new SFT data and you have rejection-sampling fine-tuning (also called RAFT / RFT / iterative SFT). It is the bridge between SFT and preference learning, and it is the most important method for this company.

Best-of-N vs training: keeping N at inference buys quality for compute, no weight change, easy to tune per request. Distilling those wins back into the weights (rejection-sampling FT) makes the model cheaper at inference and compounds over rounds, but risks narrowing diversity if you always chase the scorer's favorite region.

Part 8

RLAIF & synthetic feedback — scaling the signal

Human comparisons are the bottleneck in RLHF. RLAIF (RL from AI feedback) replaces or augments the human labeler with a model — often a stronger one — that judges or ranks candidates against a written rubric. Constitutional AI is the well-known instance: a model critiques and revises its own outputs against a set of principles, and those preferences train the policy.

  • Why it matters: it turns an expensive, slow signal into a cheap, fast one, which is the whole game in post-training. The posting lists "synthetic feedback" and "generating or curating synthetic data" explicitly.
  • The catch: an AI judge has its own biases and can be gamed; it inherits the blind spots of whatever trained it. You still need a human- or experiment-grounded audit set to know the AI judge is tracking reality.
  • The biology translation: the AI "judge" becomes an in-silico oracle — a structure predictor, a docking score, a learned fitness model. Cheap synthetic feedback you can generate at scale, grounded occasionally by the expensive wet-lab truth. This substitution is the core design move of the role.
Part 9

RLVR / GRPO — RL from verifiable rewards

When an answer can be checked — a math result, a unit test, a parseable format, a structural constraint — you don't need a learned reward model or human taste at all. The reward is the verifier's pass/fail. This is RLVR (RL from verifiable rewards), and GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, from the DeepSeek work) is the popular algorithm for it.

GRPO's trick: for each prompt, sample a group of answers, score them all, and use the group's mean as the baseline — so the advantage is just "how much better than my other attempts was this one." That removes the separate value/critic network PPO needs, cutting cost and instability.

# GRPO advantage: normalize each sample's reward within its group — no learned critic Ai = ( ri − mean(r1..G) ) / std(r1..G) # G samples per prompt; reward can be a hard verifier
The biology hook — say this one

Verifiable rewards are a natural fit for parts of biological design because many constraints are checkable without a wet lab: a designed sequence can be scored for structural validity, for not matching a hazardous template, for codon-optimization constraints, for folding to a target. Those make good verifiable-reward signals; the function (does the phage actually kill the host?) remains the expensive, non-verifiable part that only the lab resolves. Splitting objectives into "cheaply verifiable" vs "needs measurement" is exactly the kind of recipe design this role owns.

Part 10

Distillation — compressing capability

Train a smaller (or specialized) student to match a larger teacher — on its outputs (hard labels), its full probability distribution (soft labels / dark knowledge), or its rationales. Post-training's deployment valve: you get most of the capability at a fraction of the inference cost.

  • When to use: you have a strong but expensive model and need it to run cheaply, on-device, or at throughput. Also to consolidate a rejection-sampling or RLHF-improved policy into a clean checkpoint.
  • Trade-offs: the student rarely exceeds the teacher and can inherit its errors; soft-label distillation needs teacher access; task-specialized students lose generality.
  • In their work: the Omnii Defense edge model is distilled into a lightweight embedding / classification / retrieval model that runs on a laptop in the field — a textbook "capability → deployable footprint" distillation, and a concrete thing to reference.

Part 12

Data, mixtures, curriculum & forgetting

The posting puts data design on the same footing as algorithms: "study how data mixtures, objective design, curriculum, and optimization schedules affect model quality," and "improve the data we use for post-training." In practice data decisions usually move the needle more than optimizer choice.

12.1 Catastrophic forgetting and the mixture fix

Fine-tune hard on a narrow new task and the model degrades on everything else — catastrophic forgetting. The standard defense is a data mixture: blend in a replay fraction of the original distribution so the model holds its general ability while learning the new thing. Their antibody work is a clean published instance — a three-part mixture of general protein (to prevent forgetting) + public repertoires + partner designs.

12.2 Levers worth naming

  • Mixture weights: how much base / adjacent / target data, and replay fraction.
  • Curriculum: ordering (easy→hard), length schedules, when to introduce the hard modality.
  • Objective design: which loss on which head, how to weight multi-task losses, auxiliary objectives.
  • Schedules: learning-rate warmup/decay, how long at each stage, when to freeze/unfreeze the trunk.
  • Data quality: dedup, decontamination against the eval set, filtering noisy assays, balancing by cluster so one over-represented family doesn't dominate.
A line that lands

"In post-training, a data intervention — a better mixture, a decontaminated eval, a cluster-balanced sample — often beats a cleverer optimizer, and it's cheaper to verify." The posting's "nice to have" includes a time you made data quality win over modeling; have that story ready.

Part 13

Evaluating post-training

This is the highest-weighted theme in the job description — "build evaluations that actually matter," "separate real effects from artifacts, bugs, or benchmark overfitting," "identify when existing benchmarks stop being informative." Post-training specifically breaks evaluation in ways pretraining doesn't:

  • You optimized against a proxy, so you must evaluate on something else. If you trained on an RM or a surrogate scorer, reporting that same scorer's numbers is circular. Hold out an independent judge or, better, an experimental set.
  • Alignment tax: preference/safety tuning can quietly degrade raw capability. Always measure the thing you tuned and a battery of things you didn't, to catch regressions.
  • Contamination: post-training data and eval sets overlap more easily than you'd think; decontaminate deliberately.
  • Saturation & inferred labels: covered in depth in the main brief — MHC presentation at 0.939 vs 0.933 is not a decision-grade gap, and eQTL labels from SuSiE fine-mapping are statistical inferences, not measurements.
  • Decision-unit metrics: their best evaluations re-express a score as "experiments saved" or "hits per 5 slots" or "years of onset." Harder to game, immediately meaningful to a scientist.
Cross-reference

The main brief's Evaluation section has the full metric-by-metric table, the biological holdout-unit diagram, and the fine-mapping caveat written out — that is the material to pair with this page before the interview.

Part 15

Post-training Omnii — worked recipes

The rest of this brief is method theory; this part makes it concrete. These are worked, book-style examples of how post-training could and should work for Omnii-1 specifically — recipes a candidate could sketch on a whiteboard. They are illustrative teaching artifacts built from Radical Numerics' own public write-ups, not production protocols: every number cited is one they already published, and nothing here is a wet-lab instruction.

The frame that makes Omnii different from an LLM

Omnii is one shared representation across 28 genome-aligned modalities that can take assay tracks as input and predict them. Post-training it is therefore rarely "teach it to chat" — it is (a) attach a new measurement channel, (b) specialize the generative head for a design task, (c) calibrate its rankings for a decision, and (d) close a loop with a lab that returns a trickle of expensive, noisy labels. Each recipe below is one of those shapes.

15.1 A reference post-training stack for Omnii

A sensible default pipeline, with the stage boundaries chosen to protect the shared representation that makes the model valuable:

StageObjectiveWhat movesWhy here
0 · Frozen base—nothing The pretrained 28-modality trunk is the asset. Treat it as expensive and protect it.
1 · Mid-train / adaptSame self-supervised loss, shifted data new embeddings + heads full LR; trunk low LR or adapters Context extension, a new modality, or a new domain (antibodies). Mixture includes replay to prevent forgetting.
2 · Task SFTSupervised on known-good examples task head + light trunk Teach the specific output shape — a design format, a calibrated score — before any preference step.
3 · Preference / RLDPO / KTO / rejection sampling / GRPO policy head Learn "better than" from assay comparisons and from cheap verifiers. The heart of the role.
4 · Calibrate + ensembleIsotonic / temperature; rank fusion a thin calibration layer Rankings drive experiments, so the score must mean something. Fuse with orthogonal predictors.
5 · Distill (optional)Student matches teacher a smaller model Edge/throughput deployment — the Defense edge model is the published precedent.

15.2 Recipe A — teaching Omnii a modality it never saw

read Generalizing the published 5hmC diagnostics result into a repeatable recipe. 5hmC — an epigenetic mark — was absent from pretraining, yet post-training reached 0.854 allele-dosage Pearson where matched WGS coverage sat at −0.057.

  1. Exploit genome alignment. The new signal shares the model's coordinate system, so it's closer to adding a channel than learning a domain. Add a dedicated input-track embedding and an output head; leave the trunk mostly frozen (or very low LR).
  2. Self-supervised objective. Mask-and-reconstruct the new track from sequence plus the existing tracks, so no manual labels are needed and the model learns how the new mark co-varies with methylation, accessibility and conservation it already understands.
  3. Handle real-world messiness as part of the recipe. The published version imputes and phases germline SNPs (GLIMPSE2) into an individual-specific genome before conditioning — a reminder that data engineering is half of post-training.
  4. Hold out the right unit: patients, not positions. Evaluated on 32 held-out patients and 250 held-out loci. A position-level split would leak.
  5. Know when it's easy vs hard. Genome-aligned, continuous, correlated-with-existing-tracks is the easy case. A modality with no genomic coordinate (mass spec, imaging, cell-level screens) needs a different fusion mechanism and probably more trunk movement.
The generalizable principle

New modality attaches cheaply when it is aligned to a representation the model already has. That single predictor — alignment to an existing coordinate system — tells you whether to expect a frozen-trunk channel-add or a full mid-train.

15.3 Recipe B — generative design (the antibody shape)

write The hardest and most role-relevant shape: the output is a generated sequence, the domain was barely in pretraining, labels come from a lab, and designs are near-duplicates of each other.

Stage 1 — mid-train the domain in

Antibody sequence was not a dedicated pretraining domain. Use the published three-part mixture: a general-protein subset to prevent forgetting, public antibody repertoires for domain grammar, and proprietary partner designs for the specific task. The replay fraction is a tunable knob — ablate it.

Stage 2 — SFT on known binders

Teach the generative head to produce plausible, expressible candidates before any preference step. The campaign began with 1,620 designs from earlier pipelines; 150 had measured Kd — that measured slice seeds both SFT and the surrogate.

Stage 3 — build the fitness surrogate

Probe frozen representations for the traits that matter — the published ridge probes hit 0.359 expression / 0.273 potency. Keep the honest caveat in view: a one-hot baseline reached 0.246 on potency with overlapping CIs, so the surrogate's edge is real but thin. Report calibrated uncertainty, not just a point estimate — its variance drives acquisition.

Stage 4 — preference-tune from assays

Turn measurements into preference pairs holding context fixed (same target, same assay, same batch) so cross-batch calibration never enters. DPO on those pairs; or KTO if the readout is a binary "bound / didn't." Use a noise-robust variant (cDPO) because assay labels are noisy.

The split that makes or breaks it

The median design is 94% identical to its nearest neighbor. A random split leaks catastrophically — the model "learns" by memorizing a near-twin on the other side. Use cluster-aware splits (the published eval used 327 designs in 172 clusters) and cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals. If you quote a number without saying how you split, assume it's wrong.

15.4 Recipe C — constrained generation (the phage shape)

write A long genomic context with a region to redesign, where some constraints are checkable in-silico and the real objective (does it function?) needs the lab. The published gp17 tail-fiber work redesigned a receptor-binding protein with context on both sides and recovered function at 11–15 amino-acid changes 69.9% of the time vs 4.8% random.

The recipe is to split the objective by how the signal is obtained:

  • Cheaply verifiable → enforce at generation / RLVR. Structural plausibility, staying within an edit budget, codon/manufacturability rules, and screening a candidate against hazardous templates are all checkable without a lab. These make clean GRPO-style verifiable rewards and should be enforced before anything reaches a bench.
  • Experimental → rejection sampling + preference. Function itself is the expensive part. Generate many candidates, rank with the fitness predictor, send only the top to the lab, then fold results back as preference pairs and updated prioritizer. The published prioritization enriched the hard strain to 24% of the top-100 against a 4.5% base rate — a real 5×.
  • Diversity floors in every batch. Top-100 lists for different strains shared only 57 members and were disjoint for the hard strain — so a batch must not collapse onto one neighborhood, or you spend the lab budget learning nothing new.
  • Keep a random control arm every round. The 4.8% random recovery is the only reason 69.9% is interpretable, and it's the cheapest detector of a scorer drifting into self-agreement.
The safety architecture is part of the recipe, not a bolt-on

Verifiable-reward screens (hazard-template matching, capability restrictions at the data level — Evo/Evo 2 excluded eukaryotic viruses from training) and a restricted-access deployment are design choices made inside the post-training loop. Treating "is this safe to generate" as a verifiable constraint that gates generation is both the responsible and the technically cleaner framing — and a strong thing to raise given their dual mandate.

15.5 Recipe D — calibrated variant ranking (the genetics shape)

readinterpret Non-generative: the model scores how disruptive a variant is, and those scores order real clinical or experimental decisions. Post-training here is about calibration and fusion, not generation.

  • Calibrate the score (temperature / isotonic) so a percentile means the same thing across region types — the published scores are already reported as percentiles against matched gnomAD background, split by common vs rare and by region and base-change type.
  • Fuse orthogonal predictors. The best published Parkinson's result was not Omnii alone — it was reciprocal rank fusion (k=60) of Omnii + REVEL, which held the lead from rank 44 to 256. Combining a learned model with one that has different biases beats either.
  • Evaluate in decision units. Not AUROC but "−4.4 years of onset in the top 100," or "reached the known pathogenic variant at rank 22 vs 257." Those are the metrics that survive contact with a clinician.
  • Prefer experimental labels to inferred ones. The strongest genetics evals use MPRA, ADASTRA and CRISPRi — real measurements — over SuSiE fine-mapped labels, which their own footnote concedes are statistical inferences.

15.6 The active-learning loop that ties them together

Recipes B and C both bottom out in the same question: the lab can test only a handful of designs per round, so which ones? This acquisition decision — not the optimizer — is where most of the value sits, because the environment cannot be queried cheaply.

15.7 Turning noisy assay readouts into training signal

The last mile: lab data is small, batched, and noisy. How you convert it into a loss matters as much as the loss itself.

Readout you haveTurn it intoMethod
Two designs measured in the same batchA preference pair, context fixed DPO / cDPO — sidesteps cross-batch calibration entirely.
A single design labeled "worked / didn't"An unpaired binary utility label KTO — no matched pair required, which assay hits rarely come in.
A continuous value (Kd, titer, activity)A regression target for the surrogate Train the fitness model; feed its ranking to rejection sampling.
A checkable property (structure, hazard, codon rule)A verifiable reward GRPO / enforce at generation — no lab needed.
Known label noise fractionA robustified objective cDPO / rDPO that models the flip probability explicitly.
The sentence that ties Part 15 together

Post-training Omnii is active learning around a shared representation: protect the trunk, attach or specialize a head, convert whatever the lab returns into the cheapest honest signal, and spend the next round's few experiments where the surrogate is most uncertain and the designs most diverse. Every recipe above is a variation on that one loop.

Part 16

Answering technical questions

A reusable structure first, then worked examples. The structure holds for almost any post-training question: (1) name the signal and its cost, (2) pick the simplest method that fits and say why not the fancier one, (3) name the failure mode and how you'd catch it, (4) say how you'd evaluate that it worked. Interviewers are testing judgment, not recall — always justify the choice and volunteer the trade-off.

"Walk me through how you'd post-train a model for a new task."
The opener · almost certain

Start with the signal question. "First I'd ask what signal I can get and what it costs: do I have demonstrations, comparisons, a checkable verifier, or only an expensive experimental readout? That determines the method more than anything."

Then the staged default: "SFT first to get the format and basic competence — you can't do preference learning until the model produces reasonable candidates. Then, if quality exceeds what I can demonstrate, rejection sampling against the best scorer I have, because it's stable and reuses the scorer I need anyway. I'd only reach for DPO once I have clean preference pairs, and full RLHF/PPO only if I truly need on-policy exploration and can pay for the instability."

Close on evaluation: "Throughout, I'd evaluate on a held-out set independent of whatever scorer I trained against, with a proper holdout unit, and I'd watch for regressions on capabilities I didn't tune."

"DPO or RLHF — which, and why?"
Tests whether you choose for reasons

"Default DPO. It drops the reward model and the RL loop, so it's far more stable and cheaper — two models instead of four, supervised-style training. I'd pay the RLHF/PPO cost only when I specifically need on-policy improvement: the model learning from its own fresh samples and exploring beyond the fixed pair set, which off-policy DPO can't do."

Then show depth: "The honest caveat is that vanilla DPO is off-policy and can get stale, and it can push down the likelihood of both responses since it only optimizes the margin. Iterative/online DPO — regenerating pairs from the current policy — recovers much of the RLHF benefit at much lower cost, so that's usually my middle ground."

"Your model both generates designs and scores them. What goes wrong?"
Core risk in their design loops

"When the generator and scorer are the same model, rejection sampling optimizes toward the model's own confident regions — reward hacking / Goodhart. You get candidates that look great to the scorer and may be no better in reality." Cite their own data: on the hard phage strain the top-100 cleared threshold 24% of the time vs a 4.5% base rate — real 5× enrichment, but three-quarters of the confident picks still failed.

Mitigations, each tied to something they do: hold a slice of every campaign out of predictor training; keep a random control arm (the 4.8% random recovery is what makes 69.9% interpretable); enforce diversity floors so a batch can't collapse onto one neighborhood; use independent scorers where possible (the Omnii+REVEL rank-fusion ensemble is the precedent); and report calibration, not just ranking.

"How does post-training differ when the annotator is a wet lab?"
The conceptual core of the role

"Characterize the regime first: labels take days to weeks, cost real money, come in batches with assay noise, and often don't exist at any price. That rules out on-policy RL with frequent environment queries and makes it an offline, active-learning problem where the acquisition decision — which experiments to run — carries most of the value."

  • Split objectives by label cost: anything with a cheap in-silico proxy gets enforced at generation time; the expensive lab budget goes only to what truly needs measurement.
  • Prefer preference pairs over absolute rewards, holding context fixed (same strain, assay, batch) to dodge cross-batch calibration.
  • Calibrated uncertainty on the surrogate, because its variance drives acquisition, not just its mean.
  • Cluster-aware everything — with 94% median identity to nearest neighbor, naive splits and naive diversity both lie.
"When would you use RL with verifiable rewards here?"
Shows range beyond preference tuning

"Wherever the constraint is checkable without a lab — structural validity of a designed sequence, codon and manufacturability rules for an mRNA cassette, screening a design against hazardous templates, matching a target fold. Those are clean verifiable rewards, so GRPO-style RL is a good fit: group-sample, score with the verifier, normalize within the group, no learned critic. The function itself — does it actually bind or kill — stays the expensive, non-verifiable part the lab resolves. Separating the two is the recipe design."

"This benchmark looks saturated. What do you do?"
Named four separate ways in the posting

"Diagnose which of three things is happening: the metric is near ceiling, the labels aren't ground truth, or the aggregate hides the slice that matters. Ceiling → stratify until it discriminates (they slice eQTL by distance, E2G by genes skipped). Label quality → move from inferred to experimental labels (MPRA, ADASTRA, CRISPRi over SuSiE fine-mapping). Hidden slice → make the holdout unit biological. And the move I'd push hardest: re-express the metric in decision units — 'four experiments instead of five,' '1.3 of 5 vaccine slots.' Harder to game and meaningful to the scientist across the table."

"How do you prevent a fine-tune from destroying the base model's general ability?"
Data-side depth

"Catastrophic forgetting. First line of defense is a data mixture with a replay fraction of the original distribution — their antibody recipe literally includes a general-protein subset 'to prevent forgetting.' Beyond that: lower learning rates, PEFT/LoRA so the base weights move less, freezing the trunk and training new heads/embeddings (the 5hmC pattern), and always evaluating a battery of capabilities you didn't tune so you catch the regression early. I'd treat the replay fraction as a tunable knob and ablate it."

Three things to always volunteer
  • The trade-off you accepted by picking a method — never present a choice as free.
  • The failure mode and how you'd detect it — reward hacking, forgetting, contamination, off-policy staleness, saturated metric.
  • The evaluation that would tell you it actually worked, on an independent signal with a correct holdout unit.
Part 17

One-page cheat sheet

Methods, in one line each
  • SFT — imitate demonstrations; teaches format/behavior; can't exceed the data.
  • Reward model — learn a scalar from comparisons (Bradley–Terry); a cheap proxy judge.
  • RLHF/PPO — maximize reward under a KL leash; powerful, on-policy, unstable, four models.
  • DPO — preference tuning with no RM and no RL; stable, off-policy; the default.
  • KTO/IPO/ORPO/SimPO — DPO variants for binary labels / over-fitting / single-stage / reference-free.
  • Rejection sampling / BoN — sample many, keep the best, train on them; the design-loop workhorse.
  • RLAIF — an AI/oracle judge replaces the human; scales the signal, inherits biases.
  • RLVR/GRPO — RL from a verifier; group-relative baseline, no critic; for checkable answers.
  • Distillation — compress a teacher into a cheaper student; deployment valve.
Vocabulary to deploy correctly
  • KL penalty — leash keeping the policy near the reference; prevents reward hacking.
  • Reward hacking / Goodhart — optimizing a proxy until it stops reflecting the goal.
  • On- vs off-policy — learning from fresh self-samples vs a fixed dataset.
  • Catastrophic forgetting — losing general ability; fix with replay mixtures / PEFT.
  • Alignment tax — capability lost to preference/safety tuning.
  • Active learning — choosing which expensive labels to acquire; the center of this role.
  • Decision-unit metric — "experiments saved," "hits per 5 slots," "years of onset."
  • Holdout unit — the biological thing you hold out: clade, genus, chromosome, patient, cluster.
If you remember one thing

Post-training is the discipline of spending the expensive training signal only where cheaper signal can't reach. In an LLM lab the expensive signal is a human rater; at Radical Numerics it's a two-week wet-lab assay. Every method choice, mixture, and evaluation flows from that one substitution — which is why this is an active-learning and evaluation problem as much as an optimization one.
